California is considered one of the most expensive states to live in, and plenty of people have had enough. Since 2020, hundreds of thousands more people have left the state than moved here, [nearly 700,000](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-02-15/california-population-decrease-500k-2-years-9148809.html) in just the first two years of the pandemic. A lot of Californians are finally asking themselves if they can still afford to stay. Honestly, I've been asking myself the same thing.

## It starts at the pump

Gas is the first thing everyone brings up, and for good reason. We pay the most in the country, about [$5.70 a gallon](https://www.lendingtree.com/credit-cards/study/us-gas-prices) right now, while the national average is under $4. And it doesn't look like it's getting better anytime soon. Two big refineries closed in the last year, Phillips 66 in Los Angeles and Valero in Benicia, and that took about [17% of the state's refining capacity](https://www.argusmedia.com/de/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2821046-valero-ends-fuel-production-at-benicia-refinery) with it. On top of that, there's been talk of a mileage tax. It isn't real yet, and the state is [still only studying it](https://www.govbuddy.com/legislative-tracking/california-ab-1421-road-usage-charge/), but if you drive to work every day, you're paying attention.

## Then there's housing

If gas is what people complain about most, housing is what keeps them up at night.

The median price of a single-family home in California is about [$900,000](https://www.mpamag.com/us/mortgage-industry/market-updates/california-home-sales-fall-amid-mortgage-rate-spike/594114). Right now, only about [19% of California households](https://www.mpamag.com/us/mortgage-industry/market-updates/california-housing-affordability-holds-at-19-as-mortgage-rates-climb/595820) can afford a median-priced home. Put another way, more than four out of five families couldn't buy the typical house here even if they wanted to. Earlier this year, it took an income of about [$205,000 a year](https://www.ktvu.com/news/more-californians-able-afford-home-housing-affordability-hits-4-year-high-study) to afford one. In the rest of the country, it takes less than half of that.

So a lot of people rent instead, and that isn't cheap either. The average apartment in California now rents for about [$2,685 a month](https://www.rentcafe.com/average-rent-market-trends/ca). For a young person trying to save up, or a family trying to stay in the town they grew up in, that's a lot of money going out every month with nothing building up on the other side.

That's what makes the housing situation so frustrating. It isn't just that homes are expensive. It's that the path so many Californians counted on, working hard, saving up and eventually buying a place of your own, feels like it's slipping out of reach.

## What I heard on the job

Even people who already own a home aren't in the clear, because the costs don't stop at the mortgage.

When I worked in insurance, I talked to a lot of Californians, and I heard the same thing over and over. Everything keeps going up. Insurance especially. A lot of the big companies have pulled back from California, so more homeowners end up on the FAIR Plan, the state's last-resort option. Its rates are going up [an average of 30%](https://www.emarketer.com/content/more-californians-soon-priced-home-insurance) this fall.

The ones that stuck with me most were the people on Social Security who can't work anymore. Yes, Social Security goes up a little every year. In January it's going up [3.6%](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/mip/2026-10-01-f.html), which works out to about $75 a month for the average retiree. But try making $75 matter in California. That's maybe a couple tanks of gas.

And when people can't keep up, some of them end up on the street. We have more homeless people than any other state, [181,934](https://www.edhat.com/california/news/new-hud-report-finds-homelessness-declined-in-california-and-nationwide-in-2025/) in the last federal count. That number went down a little last year, but you don't have to look hard to see it.

## So why do we stay?

With all of that, you'd think everyone would be packing up. But most of us aren't, because there's still a lot to love here.

The weather is hard to beat. The people here come from everywhere, and you feel that in the food, the music and the culture. There's so much history. We have some of the best schools and universities in the world. And there's always something going on, whether it's the beach, the mountains, a game or a show.

For me, it's also just home. I was born in Newport Beach and lived there until I was about 7, then my family moved up to the Sacramento area. I live in Cameron Park now. California is the only place I've ever called home.

## Where I land

So, stay or go? I want to stay. I really do. But I'm worried about where things are headed.

We've got a new governor coming in January, and that's a real chance for things to change, on gas, on housing, on insurance, and on just being able to afford to live here. That's a big part of why I started Turnaround California. I want to keep track of what gets promised, what actually happens, and what it means for regular people like us.

If you're asking yourself the same question, I want to hear from you. Staying, leaving, not sure yet, send me your story.
